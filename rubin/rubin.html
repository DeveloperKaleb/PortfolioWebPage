    What shaped the far edge?

    Far beyond Neptune, a few objects travel on enormous, stretched orbits. Each orbit has a long axis that points somewhere. Left alone, those directions should be scattered. If something large and unseen is out there, its pull would herd them together - which is the case for a hidden planet. This section measures the herding every week, as Rubin finds more of them.

    How it works

    Rubin measures how bright a distant object is, never its size. So the size is inferred from its brightness and the albedos measured for objects as bright - or, where its own albedo has been measured, from that. The chance it was shaped by its own gravity then comes from the curve below, fitted to icy bodies whose shapes are known.

    Tundr

    This site counts a body as a planet if its own gravity has rounded it, it has a surface, and no fusion is going on in its core. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are round and have no fusion, but they have no surface at all: their gas just thins out with height. So here they are not planets. They are tundrs.

    Tundr is Old Norse for tinder. Like tinder, they are ready but never lit. That is not to say they nearly were: Jupiter would need about 80 times its mass to burn hydrogen like a star.

    Hydrogen and water. Each kind is named after what it is mostly made of. Jupiter and Saturn, the hydrogen tundrs, are about three-quarters hydrogen. Uranus and Neptune, the water tundrs, are probably mostly water, with ammonia and methane, under a thin envelope of hydrogen and helium; just how much is water rather than rock is not yet settled.

    The usual names, gas giant and ice giant, describe what they are made of by the form it took in the cold where they gathered, so a Neptune-like world right beside its star would still be an "ice giant" with no ice anywhere. These names hold wherever a tundr is found.
